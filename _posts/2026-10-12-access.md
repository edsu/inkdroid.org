I had the good fortune to attend an [Archiving the Black Web] meeting last week. The main focus of the few days in St Louis was for [fellows] to share stories of web archiving work that they had been involved with. A few themes emerged for me while listening to these presentations. We weren't using [Chatham House Rules], but I didn't ask anyone if I could write about their work here, so these are broad strokes.

* Fellows were often trying to contact website creators to negotiate consent. This process seemed like it was quite time intensive, and difficult, especially when contact needed to be established, and formal process was being followed.
* Some fellows were archiving within their existing trust networks, where consent wasn't such a big hurdle, and seemed to be established in a more informal way.
* Helping website creators understand what it meant for their websites to be archived was challenging. Communicating why they would want to have a web archive of their content was also challenging.
* There was often a desire to create new websites that arranged the web archives along with additional materials, such as oral histories, maps, and other descriptive material.
* There was also a need to limit access to a community of known users. These communities aligned with existing trust networks. I inferred from this that limiting access was one avenue to gaining consent.
* Many of the fellows were using [Browsertrix] to create web archives, and some were also using [ArchiveWebPage] to do more detailed patch crawling. It seemed like there had also been some initial early work using [Archive-It].
* When it came to integrating access to archived content, fellows were often linking to the playback mechanism inside of Browsertrix itself.
* Sometimes the website creators were given access to archived web content, and offering to serve as a backup.
* A few were thinking about the sustainability of the web archiving work in terms of having their practice become more of a service. I had an interesting side conversation about how a cooperative model could potentially help grow a web archiving practice as a form of mutual aid.

Given the interest in creating web sites for the archived web content, and administering access to them, I mentioned to one of the organizers that it would be super if the fellows could be introduced to how to embed WACZ files in their own sites using the [ReplayWebPage] web component. I learned that they were already planning on doing this, and that it just hadn't happened yet since the focus so far had been on creating the web archives. I still think [Webrecorder]'s innovation of allowing archived web content to be replayed anywhere on the web is one of their most transformative ideas.

I think it's super interesting how methods of access seems to sometimes gate the types of material, and whose material, gets archived. Understanding goals in terms of access, such as where the web archives live, and who can see them, seems to shape the types of content that can be archived? I think this has a counterpart in traditional archives where the deed of gift often stipulates how and when records can be accessed.

> Donors introduce a dynamic element to the archival process, bringing their own values, ideas, and interests. Examining donors through the concept of agency, as developed in the social sciences, reveals that they exercise significant influence on key archival functions, which in our theoretical models are generally treated as the sole purview of archival professionals. [@Fisher:2015]

I heard from at least one fellow that they were working with Omeka, and that being able to embed WACZ files in Omeka could be desirable. [Omeka S] has a [WebArchive] plugin that supports WACZ replay, but I noticed that [Omeka Classic] did not, and it is still being actively used (and maintained).

Since I had some time to fill while waiting for my plane back I "used" Claude to create a [WebArchive plugin for Omeka Classic]. It was just 80 lines of PHP, and I haven't written PHP in over a decade, so I was grateful for the help. Once you've installed the plugin you can upload WACZ files and replay them directly in Omeka, where the data is stored, and you can use Omeka's access control settings to set who can see them. Everything old is new again...

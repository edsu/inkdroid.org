The computing industry is extremely resource intensive. In terms of materials. In terms of manufacturing. In terms of energy usage. In terms of the knowledge and expertise required to build and design computer hardware. In terms of needing computers to build *the next* computers.

The list could go on, and it all was the case *before* "AI" or LLMs. AI has just thrown tons of cash and political gasoline on the already burning fire, that was already way out of control. Take a look at this [permacomputing thread] about how computing technology (the hardware) can continue to exist after economic collapse, and you will start to see this picture come into focus too.

I guess someone could say this position is a thinly disguised form of [whataboutism]. But I'm not really trying to redirect an argument against AI, so much as I'm trying to sharpen the critique of AI as a form of centralization, by zooming out and looking at the broader context of computing.

I say this because one of my [main concerns] to date about the turn to AI in *software development* has been centralization. The degree to which LLMs, and their attendant infrastructure, centralize software development is literally ỉ̫̙̿͌̂̌n͍͔̑̇ͯ͞s̬̠̰͈̗͎̑̿ͣ̓ȃ̝̬̙͇̗̗̻͜n̲͈͉̙̽ͩ͌͐e̻̓̀͗ͭ̂ͮ̔⃝. But this centralization is partly what these massive corporations want, or even need, us to believe--that the model usage **must** be centralized in order for it to work, and that they **must** be the gate keepers entrusted to let us use it.

Whether it is by design, distillation, or really by whatever means necessary, we are starting to see models being run in other places, sometimes on laptops, or on mobile devices, even in [cooperatively governed] spaces. It seems that maybe, just maybe, for software development, it might not be necessary for the model to run by \$EVIL_CORP. That it might be possible to use LLM technology to make software development easier, and more fun, more responsive to users' needs, without running every prompt through one (or a handful) of APIs. And without ingesting the entire Internet of content.

The more this decentralization (d14n) happens, the more computing returns to its [always already] massively centralized condition. Computing wasn't perfect before in terms of d14n, and pretending like it was isn't a sufficient critique of the use of LLMs in software development. However, I think it is a sufficient critique of software development more generally, and the idea of Automating All the Things just because you (think you) can ... but that is a topic for another post.

One of the eye opening things I've learned in my own (albeit limited) use of LLMs in coding has been the startling (and admittedly unwelcome) realization of how much of the work I was doing as a software developer was not creative at all. Transplanting patterns from one domain to another (conceptual copy/paste), or mundane restructuring of code, or finding bugs with unit tests. It turns out a lot of this work was *deeply repetitive*, and didn't really require much thought--or at least not as much thought as I thought it did...

But it also helped me see what work *was* creative. The biggest area for me has been being able focus on and understand what problems are worth working on. What are the approaches I could bring to an implementation? How can I understand my team members ideas and needs better? How can I understand what the people using the software need better? What areas of the codebase need attention to performance? What areas need simplification? These were always deeply satisfying things to work on *before* using LLMs in software development, but I didn't have as much time to devote to them because I was too busy indenting some code, finding a bug, moving things to use some new framework, or keeping some documentation in sync with its implementation, etc, etc.

Anyway, I guess I've found myself having to admit that LLMs are actually useful for software development, not because they make drudge work go away, but because they let you focus on the things that actually do matter.

Let me return to where I started: that computing is always, already centralized to a great degree. We should, nay we must, *always* be looking for ways to decentralize and democratize access to computing. This isn't going to happen magically. It is hard work, and requires deep levels of expertise and access to resources. But we also shouldn't deceive ourselves by not seeing how centralized computing as a technology already is, and already was, before we started prompting LLMs instead of searching Google to find the answer to a coding problem.

So how's that for whataboutism? If you see a flaw in this argument, or think it's wrong headed, please drop me a line? For what it is worth, I can't shake the feeling that this is in fact the language of addiction.

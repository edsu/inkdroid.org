Thanks for bringing up this debate and reframing it in a pretty interesting way – closer to some of the more utilitarian issues that are easily dismissed in the more well-known ethical-political context. I like the permacomputing reference.

So, I won't address all your points but I would like to make a case to "open your scull to a world that is dull", that is, to push back against your implicit argument that "creative" work is somehow superior to "repetitive" work. As someone who is increasingly (as time allows) involved with non-digital crafts, with things like hand-tool woodworking, textiles and gardening, I frequently find myself doing things that are very repetitive but still very creative. I know it might not be as glamorous to write basic CRUD things in SQL or plane a board down flat or stitch a repeating pattern. But working in those contexts give us some time to think and oftener than not, find some exceptions or details that we could only discover and consider in the moment of making the "boring bits". And those things actually matter just as much as the more intellectually impressive parts of the code as they are just as essential to the reliability and coherence of the end result.

To sum it up, I would say that by using an LLM to do the more low-status parts of code, we are denying ourselves a moment of kneeling in the weeds, working with the grain or feeling the warp and weft – things which can be repetitive, indeed, but also pleasurable while revealing specific knowledge about the medium we are working.  It's often worth the effort of doing the tedious parts the hard way even if it takes us a little bit longer.

In solidarity,

-Brendan
